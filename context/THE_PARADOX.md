# Why gradient descent works despite non-convexity in deep learning

**Gradient-based optimization reliably finds good solutions in deep neural networks even though the loss landscape is riddled with exponentially many critical points and training is provably NP-hard in the worst case.** This remains one of the deepest open questions in the theory of deep learning. The resolution appears to lie not in any single mechanism but in a convergence of structural properties — high-dimensional geometry that suppresses bad local minima, overparameterization that convexifies the problem in appropriate limits, stochastic dynamics that bias toward flat generalizing solutions, and a funnel-like landscape topology where all good solutions are connected. This report surveys the theoretical landscape surrounding this mystery with mathematical precision, drawing on statistical physics, random matrix theory, algebraic geometry, and dynamical systems.

---

## The classical guarantees that non-convexity destroys

The mystery begins with a stark contrast. Convex optimization provides ironclad guarantees: every local minimum is global, gradient descent on an $L$-smooth convex function achieves $\varepsilon$-optimality in $O(1/\varepsilon)$ iterations, and strong convexity yields linear convergence at rate $(1-\mu/L)^k$. These results, sharpened by Nesterov's optimal $O(1/k^2)$ acceleration, undergird decades of reliable machine learning via SVMs, logistic regression, and convex relaxations. In the non-convex setting, all such guarantees evaporate. Gradient descent can only promise convergence to a first-order stationary point — which could be a local maximum, saddle point, or arbitrarily bad local minimum — in $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations.

The theoretical case against training neural networks is severe. Blum and Rivest (1988) proved that deciding whether weights exist to fit a training set on a **3-node, 2-layer network** is NP-complete, via reduction from Set-Splitting. Bertschinger et al. (2023) strengthened this dramatically: training continuous-weight two-layer networks is **$\exists\mathbb{R}$-complete** — strictly harder than NP, equivalent to deciding the satisfiability of polynomial systems over the reals. Auer, Herbster, and Warmuth (1996) constructed training sequences for which a single sigmoid neuron has $2^{\Omega(n)}$ local minima. For a network with $N$ parameters, symmetry alone generates $\prod_k n_k!$ equivalent copies of each minimum through neuron permutations, and the total number of critical points can grow exponentially in dimension.

These results fueled 1990s pessimism about deep learning. Coupled with the vanishing gradient problem identified by Hochreiter (1991) and Bengio et al. (1994) — gradients through $L$ sigmoid layers decay as roughly $(0.25|w|)^L$ — the theoretical consensus held that deep networks were fundamentally untrainable. The dominance of SVMs from 1995–2010 reflected this. Then AlexNet (2012) won ImageNet by a staggering margin using straightforward SGD on a deep CNN, and the gap between theory and practice became impossible to ignore.

The crucial caveat in the hardness results is that they are worst-case. Blum and Rivest themselves noted their proofs were "fragile" — they construct pathological training sets, not typical ones. The central question thus became: **what structural properties of realistic neural network loss landscapes make them tractable despite worst-case intractability?**

## Theories of benign landscape structure and their mathematical foundations

### High-dimensional geometry kills bad local minima

The most physically grounded explanation draws on the statistical mechanics of disordered systems. Choromanska, Henaff, Mathieu, Ben Arous, and LeCun (2015) established a formal mapping between neural network loss functions and the Hamiltonian of the **$H$-spin spherical spin-glass model**, where $H$ equals the network depth. Under three assumptions — (i) independence of path-decomposed inputs, (ii) redundancy in weight parameterization, (iii) uniformity of weight-tuple multiplicities — the loss of a fully-connected ReLU network with $\Lambda$ effective parameters takes the form:

$$L_{\Lambda,H}(\tilde{w}) = \Lambda^{-(H-1)/2} \sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_H} X_{i_1,\ldots,i_H} \tilde{w}_{i_1}\cdots\tilde{w}_{i_H}$$

subject to $\Lambda^{-1}\sum_i \tilde{w}_i^2 = 1$. This is precisely the $p$-spin spherical Hamiltonian with Gaussian disorder tensor $X$.

The consequences follow from the rigorous work of Auffinger, Ben Arous, and Černý (2013), who applied the **Kac-Rice formula** to count critical points. The Kac-Rice formula transforms this counting problem into a random matrix problem: the expected number of critical points of index $k$ (having exactly $k$ negative Hessian eigenvalues) at energy level $u$ is:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{Crt}_{N,k}(u)] = 2\sqrt{\tfrac{2(p-1)}{p}} \cdot \tfrac{N}{2} \cdot \mathbb{E}^N_{\text{GOE}}\left[\exp\left(-\tfrac{N(p-2)}{2p}(\lambda^N_k)^2\right) \cdot \mathbf{1}\left\{\lambda^N_k \in \sqrt{\tfrac{p}{2(p-1)}}B\right\}\right]$$

where $\lambda^N_k$ is the $(k+1)$-th smallest eigenvalue of a GOE matrix. The asymptotic complexity $\theta_{k,p}(u) = \lim_{N\to\infty} N^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}[\text{Crt}_{N,k}(u)]$ reveals a **layered structure**: define threshold energies $E_0 > E_1 > E_2 > \cdots > E_\infty$ where $\theta_{k,p}$ first becomes positive. Below $-NE_\infty$, local minima dominate; above it, saddle points of diverging index proliferate. The probability of a high-loss local minimum is exponentially suppressed in $N$.

This is the mathematical content of the **Bray-Dean theorem** (2007): for Gaussian random fields in $N$ dimensions, critical points concentrate on a monotonic curve in the (energy, fractional index) plane. The Hessian eigenvalue density at a critical point of energy $\varepsilon$ follows a **shifted Wigner semicircle**, with the shift determined by $\varepsilon$. High-energy critical points have semicircles centered near zero (saddle points); low-energy critical points have semicircles shifted right (local minima). The key structural prediction — **that bad local minima are exponentially rare while saddle points are exponentially abundant** — has been empirically validated by Dauphin et al. (2014) on MNIST and CIFAR-10.

The assumptions underlying this mapping are, however, severely violated in practice. Real inputs are structured, not Gaussian. Path activities are correlated, not independent Bernoulli. The spherical constraint is unphysical. These limitations mean the spin-glass connection provides a compelling heuristic picture rather than a rigorous theorem about real networks.

### Escaping saddle points is computationally cheap

If most high-loss critical points are saddle points, optimization reduces to the question of efficient saddle escape. Ge, Huang, Jin, and Yuan (2015) gave the first polynomial-time guarantee: for functions satisfying the **strict saddle property** — every critical point either has large gradient, has $\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^2 f) \leq -\gamma < 0$, or is near-optimal — noisy SGD converges to a local minimum in polynomial time. Jin et al. (2017) proved the definitive result: **perturbed gradient descent** finds an $\varepsilon$-second-order stationary point (where $\|\nabla f\| \leq \varepsilon$ and $\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^2 f) \geq -\sqrt{\rho\varepsilon}$) in $\tilde{O}(\ell(f(x_0)-f^*)/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations, matching the first-order rate up to logarithmic factors. The dimension dependence is only **polylogarithmic** — $\log^4(d)$ — meaning saddle escape is essentially free.

Lee et al. (2019) proved that gradient descent with random initialization avoids strict saddle points almost surely, via the stable manifold theorem. This is elegant but asymptotic; the convergence time can be exponential without perturbation. The strict saddle property itself has been verified for matrix completion (Ge, Lee, Ma 2016), phase retrieval (Sun, Qu, Wright 2016), and tensor decomposition, but **not for general neural network training**. Kawaguchi (2016) proved that deep linear networks have no bad local minima, but showed that networks deeper than three layers harbor **degenerate saddle points** with $\lambda_{\min} = 0$ — these are not strict saddles, and no efficient escape mechanism is known.

### Overparameterization creates convex-like structure

The Neural Tangent Kernel framework (Jacot, Gabriel, Hongler 2018) shows that in the infinite-width limit under NTK parameterization, the kernel $K(x,x';\theta) = \nabla_\theta f(x;\theta)^\top \nabla_\theta f(x';\theta)$ converges to a deterministic limit $\Theta^\infty$ and remains constant during training. The dynamics become linear: $\dot{f}_t = -\eta\Theta^\infty(f_t - Y)$, with closed-form solution $f_t - Y = \exp(-\eta\Theta^\infty t)(f_0 - Y)$. The non-convex parameter-space optimization maps to **convex kernel regression** in function space.

Du et al. (2019) proved that for two-layer ReLU networks with width $m = \Omega(n^6/\lambda_0^4)$, gradient descent achieves zero training loss at linear rate. Allen-Zhu, Li, and Song (2019) extended this to deep networks: SGD finds global minima in polynomial time for fully-connected networks, CNNs, and ResNets, requiring width $m \geq \Omega(n^6 L^2)$.

The fundamental limitation was identified by Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach (2019): **NTK describes the "lazy training" regime** where parameters barely move from initialization. In this regime, no feature learning occurs — the network is equivalent to kernel regression with fixed random features. Real networks operate in the **rich/feature learning regime** where the NTK evolves during training and representations adapt to data. The NTK parameterization (weights scaled as $O(1/\sqrt{m})$) drives networks toward lazy training, while the mean-field scaling ($O(1/m)$) permits genuine feature learning. NTK theory thus explains convergence but not why deep learning outperforms kernel methods.

The **mean field alternative** (Mei, Montanari, Nguyen 2018; Chizat and Bach 2018) replaces the discrete neuron distribution with a continuous measure $\rho \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^D)$ and formulates training as the **Wasserstein gradient flow** $\partial_t\rho_t = \nabla\cdot(\rho_t\nabla_w\frac{\delta R}{\delta\rho})$. The crucial insight: the risk functional $R(\rho)$ is **convex in the linear geometry of measures**, even though the finite-particle discretization is non-convex. In the many-particle limit, propagation of chaos ensures the finite system tracks this infinite-dimensional convex optimization. Chizat (2022) proved exponential convergence of mean-field Langevin dynamics to global minimizers under a log-Sobolev inequality. Unlike NTK, mean field theory permits feature learning, but rigorous results are currently limited to two-layer architectures.

### Connected minima and landscape topology

Mode connectivity offers direct topological evidence that the loss landscape is benign. Draxler et al. (2018) used the Nudged Elastic Band algorithm from computational chemistry to find minimum-energy paths between independently trained minima of VGG, ResNet, and DenseNet on CIFAR-10/100. The paths are **essentially flat** — loss is near-constant throughout. Garipov et al. (2018) showed that simple Bézier curves with one bend suffice to connect modes at constant accuracy.

The deeper question is whether this connectivity is trivial. Frankle et al. (2020) introduced **linear mode connectivity**: networks sharing early training (from the same checkpoint) can be linearly interpolated without loss barriers, while networks from different random initializations cannot. The **Entezari et al. (2022) conjecture** proposes that all SGD solutions are linearly mode connected **after permutation alignment** — accounting for the $\prod_k n_k!$ neuron relabeling symmetry. Ainsworth et al. (2023) provided strong empirical support via their "Git Re-Basin" algorithm.

### SGD's implicit bias toward flat, generalizing solutions

Gradient descent on underdetermined problems exhibits systematic biases independent of explicit regularization. Soudry et al. (2018) proved that for logistic loss on separable data, gradient descent iterates converge in direction to the **$\ell_2$ max-margin (hard SVM) solution** at rate $O(1/\log t)$, with weight norm growing as $O(\log t)$. Gunasekar et al. (2017) showed that gradient descent on matrix factorization $X = UU^\top$ implicitly biases toward low nuclear norm (approximately low-rank) solutions, though Li et al. (2020) refined this to a greedy low-rank learning process.

The flat minima hypothesis (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997) posits that wide basins generalize better because they correspond to simpler, lower-description-length models. Keskar et al. (2017) provided empirical support: large-batch SGD finds sharp minima with poor generalization, while small-batch SGD finds flat minima with good generalization. The mechanism is the **anisotropic noise structure** of SGD (Zhu et al. 2019): when SGD is modeled as the SDE $d\theta = -\nabla L\,dt + \sqrt{(\eta/B)\Sigma(\theta)}\,dW_t$, the noise covariance $\Sigma$ aligns with the Hessian, making escape from sharp minima more efficient than isotropic Langevin dynamics would predict.

Dinh et al. (2017) mounted a devastating critique: for ReLU networks, the rescaling $W_k \to \alpha W_k$, $W_{k+1} \to \alpha^{-1}W_{k+1}$ preserves the function while making the minimum **arbitrarily sharp or flat**. Any sharpness measure not invariant to such reparameterization is theoretically vacuous. Despite this, sharpness-aware minimization (SAM, Foret et al. 2021), which solves $\min_w\max_{\|\varepsilon\|\leq\rho} L(w+\varepsilon)$, consistently improves generalization across architectures — a paradox that remains partially unresolved. Possible resolutions include PAC-Bayesian bounds that remain meaningful under specific parameterization conventions, and the observation that SGD's natural parameterization may never encounter the pathological rescalings Dinh et al. exploit.

### Lottery tickets and the structure of initialization

The lottery ticket hypothesis (Frankle and Carbin 2019) reveals that overparameterized networks contain sparse subnetworks — **"winning tickets"** at 10–20% of original size — that, when trained from their original initialization, match or exceed dense network performance. The initialization is critical: randomly re-initializing the same sparse architecture degrades performance significantly. Ramanujan et al. (2020) pushed this further: sufficiently overparameterized random networks contain subnetworks achieving competitive accuracy **without any weight training at all**, learned purely through binary mask optimization ("Edge-Popup" algorithm). This suggests optimization succeeds partly because overparameterization ensures the random initialization contains well-positioned substructures already residing in favorable landscape basins.

## Deep mathematical and physical connections

### Random matrix theory reveals the Hessian's anatomy

The Hessian spectrum of trained neural networks exhibits a characteristic **bulk-plus-outliers** structure (Sagun et al. 2017). The bulk — the vast majority of eigenvalues — clusters near zero, reflecting the massive overparameterization (flat directions in parameter space). A small number of outliers, approximately **$C-1$ to $C$ for $C$ classes**, separate from the bulk and encode data structure. Papyan (2019) decomposed the Gauss-Newton component $G$ into a hierarchical structure revealing three tiers of outliers connected to between-class and within-class gradient variations. Pennington and Bahri (2017) showed via Stieltjes transform analysis that for single-hidden-layer networks, the Hessian spectral density transitions from a **gapped Marchenko-Pastur** distribution (all eigenvalues positive, indicating a local minimum) at low loss to a gapless distribution with negative eigenvalues at high loss — the random matrix theory counterpart of the Bray-Dean prediction.

Baskerville, Granziol, and Keating (2021) found that while the mean spectral density does not precisely follow Wigner semicircle or Marchenko-Pastur distributions, **local spectral statistics** (eigenvalue spacing distributions) match GOE predictions, exhibiting the Wigner surmise $p(s) = (\pi s/2)\exp(-\pi s^2/4)$ with characteristic level repulsion. This spectral universality suggests that random matrix theory captures genuine structural features of neural network Hessians despite the non-Gaussian, data-dependent nature of the loss function.

The terminal phase of training reveals a remarkable organizing principle. Papyan, Han, and Donoho (2020) discovered **neural collapse**: as training progresses past zero error, within-class feature variability collapses to zero, class means converge to a simplex equiangular tight frame (ETF), and the classifier aligns with this same simplex. Zhu et al. (2021) proved this is the unique global optimum structure for cross-entropy with weight decay — all other critical points are strict saddles. Neural collapse thus provides a rare instance where the global minimum is both characterized and shown to be the essentially unique attractor.

### Morse theory, topology, and the piecewise-linear subtlety

Classical Morse theory connects critical point structure to landscape topology: at a non-degenerate critical point of index $k$, the sublevel set $\{f \leq a\}$ acquires a $k$-handle, changing its homotopy type. The **Morse inequalities** $c_k \geq \beta_k$ (where $c_k$ counts index-$k$ critical points and $\beta_k$ are Betti numbers) constrain the minimum topological complexity. For neural network landscapes, this framework suggests that if most critical points are high-index saddles (as the Bray-Dean picture predicts), the topology of sublevel sets changes primarily through high-dimensional handle attachments that do not disconnect the low-loss region.

Freeman and Bruna (2017) proved that for single-layer ReLU networks, **level sets become connected as width grows** — overparameterization topologically simplifies the landscape. However, they also showed that level sets become **exponentially more curved** at lower loss values, explaining the practical difficulty of navigating near-optimal regions.

ReLU networks pose a technical subtlety: they produce piecewise-linear functions for which smooth Morse theory does not directly apply. Recent work develops **PL Morse theory** where critical cells (flat regions of the canonical polyhedral complex) replace smooth critical points. Sublevel set topology can change only at non-transversal thresholds, and Betti numbers of sublevel sets serve as complexity measures for the network function.

### The algebraic geometry of parameter space

The image of the parameterization map $\phi: \Theta \to \mathcal{F}$ defines the **neuromanifold** — for polynomial activations, this is an algebraic variety. Linear networks produce **determinantal varieties** (matrices of bounded rank); shallow networks with monomial activation $\sigma(z) = z^k$ produce spaces of symmetric tensors of bounded Waring rank (Kileel et al. 2019). The quotient $\Theta/G$ by the symmetry group $G = \prod_k S_{n_k}$ is an **orbifold** with singular points where neurons coincide, and the Fisher information degenerates at these singularities. Grigsby, Lindsey, and Rolnick (2023) showed that ReLU networks possess **hidden symmetries** beyond permutation and rescaling, arising from the piecewise-linear structure, further complicating the fiber structure of the parameterization map.

Symmetry has constructive consequences. Simsek et al. (2021) proved that adding a single extra neuron per layer suffices to **connect all discrete symmetry-related minima into a single continuous manifold**, converting the combinatorial proliferation of equivalent minima into a connected solution set. Zhao et al. (2022) identified **conserved quantities** under gradient flow for 2-homogeneous networks (e.g., $\|W_{\text{out}}\|^2 - \|W_{\text{in}}\|^2$ is preserved) and showed that **symmetry teleportation** — exploiting group actions to jump between equivalent points — can accelerate optimization.

### The single-funnel picture from energy landscape theory

The most integrative perspective comes from chemical physics. Ballard, Das, Martiniani, Mehta, Sagun, Stevenson, and Wales (2017) applied Wales's energy landscape toolkit — disconnectivity graphs, basin-hopping, kinetic transition networks — to neural network loss functions. The central finding: overparameterized classification networks exhibit a **single-funnel landscape**, where all local minima occupy a narrow energy band and are connected through low-barrier transition states. This directly parallels the **funnel hypothesis** in protein folding (Bryngelson and Wolynes 1987): naturally evolved proteins fold reliably because their energy landscapes are funneled toward the native state, overcoming the Levinthal paradox of $10^{300}$ conformations.

The neural network analogue of Levinthal's paradox — why SGD navigates an exponentially large parameter space in polynomial time — resolves through the same mechanism. The funnel steepness $\delta E$ (energy bias toward good solutions) overwhelms the roughness $\Delta E$ (amplitude of local traps). Increasing training data transforms the landscape from multi-funnel to single-funnel, paralleling the distinction between "good folders" and "glass formers" in protein biophysics. The mode connectivity results of Draxler et al. and Garipov et al. are the empirical signature of this single-funnel structure: all good solutions lie in one connected basin.

### The Kac-Rice formula and the replica method as unifying calculus

Two computational tools from statistical physics undergird much of this theory. The **Kac-Rice formula** transforms critical point counting into a random matrix expectation:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{Crit}_{f,k}(B)] = \int_M d\mu_M(x) \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[|\det\text{Hess}\,f(x)|\cdot\mathbf{1}\{f(x)\in B,\, i(\text{Hess}\,f)=k\}\,\big|\,\text{grad}\,f(x)=0\right]\cdot\varphi_{\text{grad}\,f(x)}(0)$$

For the $p$-spin model, the joint distribution of $(f, \text{grad}\,f, \text{Hess}\,f)$ has the remarkable property that $f$ and $\text{Hess}\,f$ are independent of $\text{grad}\,f$, making the conditional expectation tractable. The Hessian conditioned on being at a critical point of value $u$ equals $\sqrt{2(N-1)p(p-1)}\,M_{N-1} - pu\cdot I_{N-1}$ where $M_{N-1}$ is GOE — the shifted semicircle of Bray-Dean.

The **replica method** addresses a complementary question: the typical volume of weight space compatible with correct classification. Gardner (1988) computed this for perceptrons via the identity $\langle\ln V\rangle = \lim_{n\to 0}(\langle V^n\rangle - 1)/n$, obtaining under the replica-symmetric ansatz:

$$N^{-1}\langle\ln V_\kappa\rangle = \text{extr}_q\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1-q) + \tfrac{q}{2(1-q)} - \alpha\int Dz\,\ln H\!\left(\tfrac{\kappa + \sqrt{q}\,z}{\sqrt{1-q}}\right)\right\}$$

This yields the celebrated capacity $\alpha_c(0) = 2$ (a perceptron with $N$ weights classifies at most $2N$ random patterns), confirmed rigorously. The replica approach extends to study phase transitions in learning — discontinuous jumps in generalization error at critical sample sizes, symmetry-breaking transitions where hidden units specialize, and the jamming transition between under- and overparameterized regimes.

## Conclusion

The mystery of non-convex optimization in deep learning is not resolved by any single theory but by their collective implication: **the loss landscape of overparameterized networks is far more structured than a generic non-convex function**. The spin-glass mapping and Kac-Rice calculations show that high-loss critical points are overwhelmingly saddle points, not local minima. Overparameterization, formalized through NTK and mean field theories, converts the problem into a convex optimization in appropriate infinite-dimensional limits. Mode connectivity and energy landscape analysis reveal a single-funnel topology where all good solutions are connected. SGD's anisotropic noise biases optimization toward flat, generalizing regions, while the implicit bias of gradient descent selects structured solutions (max-margin, low-rank) without explicit regularization.

The deepest gap in current understanding is the **feature learning regime**: NTK theory explains convergence but not why deep learning outperforms kernels, while mean field theory permits feature learning but is limited to shallow architectures. The Choromanska spin-glass mapping relies on independence assumptions that real networks violate. The flat minima narrative remains theoretically contested despite SAM's empirical success. A fully rigorous theory explaining why SGD on a finite-width deep network, trained on structured data, finds solutions that generalize — one that unifies the NTK, mean field, spin glass, and mode connectivity perspectives — remains the central open problem in deep learning theory. What we can say with increasing confidence is that the combination of high dimensionality, overparameterization, stochastic dynamics, and architectural symmetry conspires to create landscapes that are, in a precise statistical sense, benign.
